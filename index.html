    PhD Candidate · Machine Learning · Georgia Tech

    Andrew Rosemberg

    I work at the intersection of machine learning and mathematical optimization, developing methods for reliable, effective, and computationally efficient decision-making under uncertainty.

    My research centers on the relationship between representations, decision policies, and computation. I study how policies and solution maps can be learned, approximated, and integrated with mathematical decision methods, exploiting information across problem instances while retaining the ability to adapt to individual circumstances. A central concern is how these choices affect generalization, decision quality, and the computational resources required to achieve them.

    This perspective extends to decisions made under incomplete information, uncertainty estimated from data, and interactions with other agents. I investigate these questions through mathematical analysis, learning algorithms, and open-source computational tools.

    Learning solution maps

    Repeated computation creates an opportunity to learn structure across context, without treating every instance as an unrelated problem.

    A parametric procedure induces a map from context to decisions. Learning that map amortizes the cost of repeated solution, but transfers the burden of generalization from the procedure to its representation. The relevant approximation must retain the local structure that governs decisions in new contexts and the response required when it becomes one component of a larger system.

    Learning Solution Maps →

    Policies for multistage decisions

    When decisions alter the context of later decisions, computation must be allocated across time rather than repeated myopically.

    A policy is a solution map evaluated along the trajectory it creates. Its representation determines what is prescribed in advance, what is computed after new information arrives, and how future consequences enter the current decision. Appropriate reformulation can expose separability, preserve prior structure, and make reliable policy learning compatible with parallel computation.

    Policies for Multistage Decisions → Continuation-Value Representations → Planning and Realization Mismatch →

    Multi-level decision problems

    A decision is often evaluated through a mechanism whose response is itself part of the problem.

    Parameters can be interpreted by a model, targets can be realized by a downstream process, and the outcome of one decision can depend on the response of others. These systems are coupled even when their component objectives differ. The relevant learning signal is determined by the response of the composite decision process, not by any learned component in isolation.

    Differentiating Optimization Models → Endogenous Response → Learned Model Correction →

    Uncertainty and ambiguity

    Data provide evidence about future context; they do not identify the law on which a policy is conditioned.

    Selection against an estimated model favors decisions whose apparent performance is helped by estimation error. Ambiguity is therefore a property of the conditional law used to construct a policy, not merely a perturbation of its eventual outcome. The research studies data-informed uncertainty sets and the consequences of mismatch between the model used for a decision and the mechanism that realizes it.
